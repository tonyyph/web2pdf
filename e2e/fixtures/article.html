The Quiet Engineering Behind Paper Sizes

By A. Reviewer · 21 September 2026 · 8 min read

Every A4 sheet you have ever handled is a rectangle whose sides stand in a ratio of one to the square root of two. That is not an aesthetic choice. It is the only ratio that survives being folded in half: halve an A4 sheet across its long side and you get two sheets of exactly the same proportion, which we call A5.

Why the ratio matters

Because the proportion is preserved, scaling artwork between sizes never crops or letterboxes it. A poster designed for A2 reduces cleanly onto A4 with a single percentage, and every photocopier in the world ships with that percentage as a labelled button.

A standard is successful when it stops being visible. Nobody thinks about the square root of two while loading a printer tray.

The numbers

Size Millimetres Inches
A3 297 × 420 11.69 × 16.54
A4 210 × 297 8.27 × 11.69
Letter 215.9 × 279.4 8.5 × 11
Legal 215.9 × 355.6 8.5 × 14

The American exception

US Letter is 8.5 by 11 inches, a ratio of roughly 1.294 rather than 1.414. Halving it does not reproduce itself, which is why American office workflows involve rather more manual scaling than they might otherwise.

A note on the B and C series

The C series sizes envelopes: a C4 envelope swallows an unfolded A4 sheet, and a C5 takes an A4 folded once. The B series sits geometrically between adjacent A sizes.

What this has to do with software

Printing from a browser means reconciling a document with no fixed height against a sheet that has one. The page box, its margins, and the scale factor are the three dials that reconciliation turns, and every one of them is expressed in inches once it reaches the printing pipeline, regardless of what the interface showed you.

A converter that respects this does its arithmetic once, in one place, and keeps the rounding consistent. One that does not produces PDFs whose margins drift by a millimetre or two depending on which dialog you came through.
